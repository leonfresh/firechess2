If you're serious about improving at chess, you've probably asked yourself: **should I use Lichess or Chess.com?**

It's one of the most debated questions in online chess. Both platforms have millions of active players, both offer analysis tools, puzzles, and learning resources — and both claim to help you improve. But they take fundamentally different approaches, and the right choice depends on your goals, budget, and learning style.

In this deep-dive comparison, we'll look at every feature that matters for improvement: analysis depth, puzzle quality, learning tools, opening preparation, game review, community, mobile experience, and price. By the end, you'll know exactly which platform fits your improvement journey.

Let me be upfront: this is FireChess's blog, and we're a smaller platform. But that lets us give you an honest, unbiased comparison — we're not competing with either Lichess or Chess.com. Our goal is to help you make the right choice, and if neither platform covers a specific need (like [Chaos Chess](https://firechess.com/chaos) or certain [analysis features](https://firechess.com/analysis)), we'll mention that too.

## The Big Picture: Two Philosophies of Chess Online

Before we compare features one by one, it's worth understanding the philosophical divide between these two platforms.

**Lichess** is open-source, non-profit, and completely free. Every feature — analysis, puzzles, studies, opening explorer, engine evaluation — is available without paying a cent. The trade-off is that the user interface feels more utilitarian, and the learning resources (video lessons, structured courses) are thinner. Lichess trusts you to explore and figure things out. Their mantra is "we do it for the chess."

**Chess.com** is a for-profit company with the largest user base in online chess. The free tier is quite limited, but the paid plans ($6.99–$14.99/month) unlock a massive library of video lessons, interactive courses, computer opponents, and the AI-powered "Game Review" feature. The interface is polished, gamified, and designed to keep you engaged. Their approach is "we'll guide your improvement."

Here's the key insight: **Lichess gives you better raw tools for improvement, but Chess.com gives you a better structured path.** Which one works for you depends on whether you're self-directed or prefer guidance.

## Analysis Tools: Raw Power vs Guided Feedback

The analysis board is where most improvement happens. You play a game, review it with the engine, find your mistakes, and learn from them. This is arguably the single most important feature for rating growth.

### Lichess Analysis

Lichess offers a full-featured analysis board with Stockfish 18 (the latest version of the strongest chess engine in the world). You can step through any game, see engine evaluations in centipawns, view the top engine lines, and toggle the evaluation graph. All of this is free, unlimited, and available in your browser or mobile app.

One of Lichess's standout features is **Cloud Analysis**. When you finish a game, Lichess runs it through Stockfish on their servers and highlights every mistake, inaccuracy, and blunder with a color-coded bar. The computer analysis is nearly instantaneous for most positions because the results are cached from thousands of previous analyses. You get unlimited cloud analysis for free.

The **"Learn from Your Mistakes"** feature takes your analyzed games and builds a custom training session around the exact positions where you went wrong. This is incredibly powerful: instead of doing generic puzzles, you're drilling the specific tactical patterns and positional errors you make in your own games. It's free and integrated directly into the analysis flow.

### Chess.com Analysis

Chess.com's analysis board is also powered by Stockfish, but the depth is tied to your membership level. Free users get limited analysis — you can see basic evaluations but the deep lines and full game review are locked behind the paywall.

The flagship feature is **Game Review**, which is Chess.com's AI-powered analysis tool. It doesn't just show you engine evaluations — it explains *why* a move was good or bad in plain English. For example, instead of showing "+1.2" it might say "This move hangs your knight to Qxh7+." The coach provides context and teaches you the reasoning behind engine lines.

Chess.com's analysis is more accessible to beginners who don't know how to interpret engine evaluations. It translates engine numbers into actionable advice. The downside: you're limited to a certain number of Game Reviews per day on the free tier (usually one or two), and even on paid plans, the analysis isn't as deep as Lichess's cloud engine (Chess.com limits search depth on cheaper plans).

### Verdict on Analysis

**Winner: Lichess** — for sheer analytical depth and unlimited access. If you know how to read engine evaluations (and you should learn — it's not that hard), Lichess gives you more power. Chess.com's Game Review is better for absolute beginners who need guidance, but power users will prefer Lichess's unrestricted access to Stockfish 18.

## Puzzles: Training Your Tactical Eye

Tactical puzzles are the fastest way to improve your chess. Both platforms offer them, but the approach differs significantly.

### Lichess Puzzles

Lichess has an infinite stream of puzzles sourced from real games played on the platform. Every puzzle is a tactical position where one side has a forced winning sequence. The system adjusts to your rating, and the puzzle pool is enormous — over 3 million puzzles at the time of writing.

The key features:
- **Unlimited puzzles for free** — no daily cap
- **Puzzle themes** (over 40 themes including forks, pins, sacrifices, endgames)
- **Puzzle Streak** — solve as many as you can in a row
- **Puzzle Storm** — solve as many as you can in 3 minutes
- **Puzzle Racer** — head-to-head puzzle solving
- **Puzzle Dashboard** — shows your strengths and weaknesses by theme

The downside: because puzzles are sourced automatically from real games, the quality is occasionally uneven. A small percentage of puzzles have multiple winning moves or unclear solutions.

### Chess.com Puzzles

Chess.com's puzzles are curated by their team. Each puzzle guarantees a single best move sequence, and the quality control is noticeably higher. The adaptive rating system works well, and the interface is polished.

The key features:
- **3 free puzzles per day** on the free tier — unlimited on Diamond
- **Puzzle Rush** — solve as many as you can against the clock
- **Puzzle Battle** — real-time head-to-head puzzle solving
- **Custom Puzzles** — practice specific themes
- **Video explanations** for many puzzles (paid tier)

Chess.com's big advantage is the **curated quality** and **video explanations** that walk you through the solution. If you're stuck on a hard puzzle, you can watch a GM explain the key ideas. This is genuinely valuable for learning.

### Verdict on Puzzles

**Tie** — with a caveat. Lichess wins on volume, variety, and price (free unlimited). Chess.com wins on quality control and teaching value. If you're a beginner, Chess.com's curated puzzles with video explanations will teach you more. If you're intermediate or advanced and need high-volume pattern recognition training, Lichess is better.

## Learning Resources: Structured vs Self-Directed

This is where the two platforms diverge most sharply.

### Lichess Learning

Lichess takes a minimalist approach to learning content:

- **Learn** — interactive lessons on chess basics (rules, piece movement, basic tactics)
- **Practice** — drilling specific endgame and tactical patterns
- **Coordinate** — a tool to improve your board vision and square recognition
- **Studies** — user-created, shareable analysis documents (think collaborative research papers for chess positions)

Studies are Lichess's secret weapon. Anyone can create a Study with multiple chapters, annotated variations, embedded puzzles, and diagrams. The Lichess community has created thousands of Studies covering everything from opening repertoires to famous games to complex endgame theory. It's like Wikipedia for chess analysis — but you have to know what you're looking for.

Lichess also has a **Video Library** with embedded YouTube content, but it's a simple directory rather than a structured curriculum. You browse by channel or topic and watch whatever catches your interest.

### Chess.com Learning

Chess.com has invested heavily in structured learning content:

- **Lessons** — hundreds of interactive video lessons organized by topic and skill level, from complete beginner (rating 400) to advanced (2000+)
- **Guided Courses** — multi-lesson paths designed by GMs and titled coaches
- **Video Library** — over 3,000 hours of high-production chess content, including commentary, analysis, and instruction from top GMs
- **Drills** — position-specific repetition training for openings, endgames, and tactics
- **Opening Explorer with Video** — every major opening line has an accompanying video explanation

The big advantage is **structure.** If you're rated 1200 and don't know what to study next, Chess.com will recommend a learning path that matches your level. It's like having a coach who gradually increases the difficulty.

The downside: almost all of this is locked behind the Diamond membership ($14.99/month). The free tier gives you a few introductory lessons and then hits a paywall.

### Verdict on Learning

**Winner: Chess.com** — but only if you pay. Their structured curriculum is genuinely excellent for guided improvement, especially for players under 1800 who benefit from a clear progression. Lichess Studies are more powerful for advanced players who know what they need to study, but the lack of structure makes them less useful for beginners.

## Opening Preparation: Two Approaches

Opening study is an essential part of improvement, especially once you cross 1200.

### Lichess Opening Explorer

Lichess's opening explorer is based on over 6 billion games from the platform. You enter any position, and it shows you the most common moves, win rates, average ratings, and performance statistics. It's incredibly fast and comprehensive.

The **Personal Opening Explorer** is even better: connect it to your Lichess account, and it shows you exactly which lines you've played, your win rate in each, and where you deviate from main theory. This is a phenomenal tool for building an opening repertoire.

Lichess also includes a **7-piece endgame tablebase** — perfect tablebase results for any position with 7 or fewer pieces on the board. This is a powerful tool for endgame study that Chess.com doesn't offer for free.

### Chess.com Opening Explorer

Chess.com's opening explorer is integrated into the analysis board and shows statistics from games on their platform. It's solid, but the free tier limits how many positions you can explore per day (typically 5-10).

The paid advantage: Chess.com's opening explorer includes **GM video explanations** for the most common lines. If you want to know why the Open Sicilian is preferred over the Closed Sicilian against the Najdorf, there's a video from a titled player explaining the strategic ideas.

### Verdict on Opening Preparation

**Winner: Lichess** — the combination of unlimited access, massive game database, personal explorer, and endgame tablebase makes it the superior tool for opening study. Chess.com's video explanations add value, but the Lichess free offering is simply more practical for daily use.

## Chess Engines: Stockfish Showdown

Both platforms use Stockfish, but there are meaningful differences in how it's deployed.

| Feature | Lichess | Chess.com (Free) | Chess.com (Diamond) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engine version | Stockfish 18 | Stockfish 16 | Stockfish 17 |
| Cloud analysis | Unlimited | 1 game/day | 30 games/day |
| Local analysis | Unlimited (browser) | Limited depth | Unlimited |
| Evaluation lines | Top 5 variations | Top 3 variations | Top 5 variations |
| Endgame tablebase | 7-piece (free) | 7-piece (free) | 7-piece (free) |
| Analysis depth | 25+ ply | ~18 ply | ~22 ply |

**Winner: Lichess** — you get the latest Stockfish with the deepest analysis, for free, with no caps.

## Community and Matchmaking

Improvement isn't just about studying — you need to play regularly against opponents at your skill level.

### Lichess Matchmaking

Lichess uses a Glicko-2 rating system, which is slightly different from Chess.com's Glicko-1 variant. The matchmaking is fast at most rating levels, and you can queue for multiple time controls simultaneously. The Arena tournaments run around the clock and are an excellent way to practice under tournament conditions.

The rating system is somewhat stricter — Lichess ratings are typically 150-300 points lower than Chess.com ratings for the same player. This means a 1500 on Lichess is roughly equivalent to a 1700-1800 on Chess.com. Don't let your Lichess rating discourage you — it's not lower because you're worse, it's because the system is calibrated differently.

**Cheating detection** on Lichess is widely considered the best in online chess. Their automated detection catches most cheaters quickly, and tournaments are well-protected.

### Chess.com Matchmaking

Chess.com has a larger player pool, so matchmaking is faster at all levels. The rating system is more lenient, which means faster rating gains early on. The platform runs daily tournaments, and the "Play Coach" feature lets you get live feedback from titled players (paid feature).

Chess.com also offers **Variants** — a growing selection of chess variants that add fun and tactical creativity. While Lichess has more variants (by a wide margin), Chess.com's variants are more polished and more heavily played.

### Verdict on Community

**Winner: Chess.com** — larger player pool means faster matchmaking, especially at extreme rating levels (below 400 or above 2500). The daily tournaments and events are more engaging. But Lichess has better anti-cheating and more variants.

## Mobile Experience

Both platforms have excellent mobile apps, available on iOS and Android.

### Lichess Mobile

Lichess's mobile app is open-source and ad-free. It includes all core features: play, puzzles, analysis, studies, and tournaments. The interface is clean but minimal — more functional than beautiful. The app is lightweight and fast, and it includes offline play for when you're without internet.

### Chess.com Mobile

Chess.com's mobile app is more polished with better animations and a more modern design. The app integrates all features including lessons, video library, and clubs. The push notification system is more sophisticated (daily puzzle reminders, tournament alerts). The downside: ads on the free tier, and some features (like lessons and Game Review) require a paid membership.

### Verdict on Mobile

**Winner: Chess.com** — the app is simply more polished. But Lichess's app is perfectly adequate and has the advantage of being ad-free and fully free.

**The bottom line:** If you're on a tight budget, Lichess is the clear winner. If you can afford $10-15/month for Chess.com Diamond, the structured learning content adds real value for improvement, especially at intermediate levels.

To make this practical, here's what we'd recommend based on your current level:

### Below 1000 (Beginner)

**Start with Chess.com's free tier**, then upgrade to Diamond if you can afford it. At this level, you benefit most from structured lessons that explain basic concepts. The Game Review feature will help you understand why your moves are mistakes. Focus on playing longer time controls (15+10 or longer) and doing 10-15 puzzles per day.

## The Verdict

After comparing every major feature across both platforms, here's our honest take:

**Choose Lichess if:** you're on a budget, you know how to use engine analysis, you prefer deep analytical tools over guided instruction, and you value unlimited access to everything.

**Choose Chess.com (Diamond) if:** you're below 1500 and need structured guidance, you prefer video instruction over reading, you want an all-in-one platform with a polished experience, and the monthly cost is worth it to you.

**Use both if:** you're serious about improvement. Lichess for analysis, puzzles, and opening study. Chess.com for playing, structured lessons, and the larger community. This is the most effective combination.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are Lichess ratings comparable to Chess.com ratings?

No. Lichess and Chess.com use different rating systems (Glicko-2 vs Glicko-1) and have different player pools, so the numbers aren't directly comparable. A Lichess rating is typically 150–300 points lower than the equivalent Chess.com rating. A 1500 on Lichess roughly corresponds to a 1700–1800 on Chess.com. Don't compare raw numbers between platforms — track your improvement within a single platform instead.

### Can I import my games between Lichess and Chess.com?

Yes. Both platforms let you export games as PGN files, and both support importing PGNs from the other. You can download your entire game history from either site (Settings → Export on Chess.com, or the API/game download on Lichess) and upload it to the other. This is useful if you want to switch platforms but keep your game history, or if you want to analyze Chess.com games with Lichess's free engine.

### Is Chess.com worth paying for if I already use Lichess?

It depends on your level and learning style. Below 1500, Chess.com's structured lessons and Game Review feature genuinely add value that Lichess doesn't replicate — guided video instruction and plain-English explanations of your mistakes. Above 1500, the gap narrows significantly because you can interpret engine analysis on your own. If you're budget-conscious, Lichess alone covers 90%+ of what you need for improvement. The Chess.com Diamond subscription is worth it primarily for the curated learning content and larger tournament pool.

### Which platform has better anti-cheat detection?

Both platforms invest heavily in anti-cheating, but Lichess is generally considered to have the stronger automated detection system. Lichess's open-source anti-cheat engine catches most engine-assisted players quickly, and their tournament protection is highly rated by the community. Chess.com also has robust detection, and their larger staff means manual reviews are more common. In practice, both platforms handle cheating reasonably well at standard time controls, though faster time controls (bullet, blitz) are harder to police on either platform.

### Do professional and titled players prefer one platform over the other?

It's split. Many top GMs stream and play on Chess.com because of sponsorship deals and the larger casual audience. However, a significant number of titled players — including some of the world's best — actively use Lichess for serious training because of the superior analysis tools and open-source transparency. Magnus Carlsen has been associated with Chess.com, while many strong European and Asian players prefer Lichess. For improvement purposes, the platform a titled player uses doesn't matter as much as the training habits they describe. Both platforms are used at the highest levels.
